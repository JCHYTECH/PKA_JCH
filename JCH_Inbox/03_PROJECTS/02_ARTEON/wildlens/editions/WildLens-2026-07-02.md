## ① The Frame — The Nest That Tells the Truth

*Canon EOS R10 · RF 200-800mm F6.3-9 IS USM · © JC Havaux / ARTEON*

A Northern Gannet (*Morus bassanus*) sits on its nest. The nest should be made of seaweed, grass, feathers — the soft, organic materials that gannets have gathered for millennia on cliff-ledges and rocky islands. This one is made of fishing net.

Not one piece. Not a thread. The entire structure — the cup, the rim, the lining — is synthetic debris pulled from the sea and woven into a shape that the bird's instincts still recognise as "nest," even though the material would not exist in any world that hadn't been industrialised and trawled and discarded into.

The gannet has adapted. That is what animals do. They use what is available. They rebuild with what we leave behind. A gannet does not distinguish between a strip of polypropylene netting and a blade of eelgrass — both are flexible, both are weaveable, both hold the egg in place. The adaptation is real, it is functional, and it is heartbreaking.

Because this is not a story of resilience. It is a story of substitution. The fishing net that forms this nest was once part of an industrial operation that removed the fish from the waters around this colony. The net caught the fish. The net was lost or discarded. The net washed up on the colony. The gannet took it and built with it. The same industry that emptied the sea provided the material for the nest that replaces what the sea no longer gives.

We — photographers, naturalists, viewers — stand in front of this image and we feel something. The image works because it shows adaptation, yes. But it also shows the price. The gannet looks healthy. The nest looks functional. And yet everything in this frame — the bird, the nest, the cliff it sits on — exists in a world that has been fundamentally altered by human activity, and the bird is surviving in the ruins of its own ecosystem by repurposing the wreckage.

This is what wildlife photography can do that no statistic can: make the invisible visible. A number — "X tonnes of fishing gear lost annually" — passes through the mind. An image of a gannet sitting on a nest of netting stays there. It does not argue. It shows. And showing, as Arteon's manifeste says, is *« modestement, recoudre. »*

This photograph will not stop ghost fishing. It will not end marine plastic pollution. But it does something that data cannot: it makes the viewer *see* the adaptation as loss. The gannet is not thriving. The gannet is coping. There is a difference, and photography is one of the few tools that can make that difference legible.

## ② The Shift — Content Credentials: The Infrastructure of Honesty

A quieter development this week, but one that matters for anyone who photographs the wild and wants the image to be believed.

The C2PA — the coalition behind Content Credentials — announced version 2.3 of their provenance standard in February 2026, with a conformance program designed to make credentials more reliable across the image ecosystem. Adobe's Content Authenticity tools can now inspect and embed provenance metadata across DNG, HEIC, JPEG, PNG, TIFF, AVIF, and WebP.

What this means: an image can now carry a cryptographically signed record of its creation and editing history — camera model, lens, timestamp, edits applied — that travels with the file rather than depending on a platform's willingness to display it.

For wildlife photography, this matters more than it sounds. We live in an era where the question "was this real?" is becoming default. AI generation, compositing, and silent manipulation tools are ubiquitous. The gannet photograph above carries its own weight because the nest is visible — but not every image of environmental damage is this obvious. Some images show subtler truths: a habitat fragmented by a road, a population thinner than last year, a behaviour shifted by human pressure. Those images need to be trusted, and trust needs infrastructure.

The technology exists. The bottleneck is adoption. Photographers who start embedding Content Credentials now — before it becomes mandatory, before the audience demands it — are building the credibility that will matter when the flood of AI-generated nature imagery makes provenance the only differentiator.

If you photograph the wild, sign your work. Not with a watermark. With its history.

## ③ The Lens — When the Camera Listens: Photography as Ecological Sensor

There is a shift happening in conservation biology that wildlife photographers should know about, because it changes what a photograph is *for*.

For most of its history, wildlife photography has been understood as a visual art: the image is the deliverable, the print is the endpoint. But a growing body of work in ecological monitoring is treating the camera — and increasingly, the microphone — as a **sensor**: a field instrument that records biological data continuously, without a human behind it.

Camera traps have been used this way for decades. What is new is the scale and the intelligence applied to the output. Networks of motion-triggered cameras now cover reserves, migration corridors, and restored habitats — not to produce beautiful images, but to estimate population density, detect invasive species, and measure the rate at which a recovering ecosystem repopulates. The photographs are data points. AI models trained on millions of camera-trap images can identify species, count individuals, and flag behavioural anomalies faster than any human reviewer.

The same logic is now extending to sound. Passive acoustic monitoring — deploying autonomous recorders in forests, wetlands, and marine environments — captures the acoustic signature of an ecosystem over weeks or months. BirdNET, developed at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, can identify bird species from audio in real time. Similar models are being trained for bats, frogs, and insects. The sound of a healthy forest at dawn is not just beautiful. It is a census.

**What this means for photographers:** the boundary between "making images" and "producing ecological knowledge" is dissolving. A well-placed trail camera does not replace a photographer — no algorithm composes with intent, reads light for emotional weight, or chooses the moment that tells a story a human needs to hear. But the photographer who understands that their camera is also a sensing instrument — that the same patience, fieldcraft, and location knowledge that produces a great image also produces meaningful data — is operating in a richer territory than the one who chases only the frame.

The gannet photograph in this edition is a portrait. It is also evidence. It documents a behaviour — nest-building with synthetic debris — that is occurring across gannet colonies worldwide. A researcher studying marine plastic pollution could use this image as a record. A conservation NGO could use it in a campaign. A museum could exhibit it as testimony. The photograph does not stop being art. But it becomes more than art — it becomes an instrument.

This is the territory WildLens will explore: photography not as an end, but as a node in a network of seeing, listening, and understanding what is happening to the wild.
