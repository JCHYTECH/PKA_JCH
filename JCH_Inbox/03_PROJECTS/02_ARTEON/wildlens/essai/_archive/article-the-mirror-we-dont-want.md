# The Mirror We Don't Want

> *It is not necessary for Eagles to be Crows.*
> — Sitting Bull (Hunkpapa Lakota)

They promise us they will translate the animals. AI, soon, will know what the dog means, what the whale is telling us. The question is asked on the radio, in the papers, with the same word: at last.

I don't believe it — but not for the reason you'd expect.

The dream of translation is not a wish to listen. It is a wish to be heard. We don't want to know what the animal thinks; we want it to confirm what we think of ourselves. That it loves us. That we are good. That we matter.

A caller says it better than I can, on the air, without knowing it: we must not ask our animals to make up for our own unease. A translation machine is a projection machine. It will not reveal the animal; it will reveal what we put into it.

And even if it worked, we should refuse. Because the bond rests precisely on what does not translate.

A man phones the show. He has dogs. They ask if he dreams of understanding them better. He answers: I don't need it. And he tells them — his dog looking at him, once the object to be found is gone. "She has a message in her eyes. You can't get it wrong."

There it is. That message passes through no language and needs none. The strength of the bond is not a failure of translation — it is its condition. To translate would be to break it. The man says it himself: "it would break something."

This is not drawing-room intuition. History has already shown it, at the scale of a continent.

When they set out to "understand" the American Indian, they did not understand him: they translated him into their own frame, then called it understanding. His religion became idolatry, his land property, his kinship barbarism. And to "save" him, they had to kill him. The formula survives, dated 1879: *Kill the Indian, save the man.*

The dream of translating the animal has the same shape, in four words: kill the animal to save the animal. The Other, killed, then handed back — domesticated, translated, in our image. As if the eagle were nothing until it became a crow.

The animal is not a colonized people. I say this to be fair: the American Indian was a fully human Other, with a whole language. The animal is a farther Other still — a world we only perceive at its edge. Which is exactly why the gesture, on it, is more radical: it cannot even answer back in our frame. The erasure is total, and silent.

And the ethologist, on the air, drops the sentence no one picks up: if we gave animals a voice, they would begin by reproaching us. We would be disappointed to hear what they have to say about us.

That is the reverse of the question. We do not fear the silence. We fear the answer. We are not trying to hear the animal; we are trying to hear it absolve us.

It gets worse. Even the attempt has a cost. Running machines to decode the living means burning energy — and the habitat that energy degrades belongs to the very ones we claim to listen to. To hear the living, we burn it. The dream of translation is its own first damage.

The animal stays silent. It is not a lack; it is a restraint. It could judge us — and it refrains. Its gaze, when it settles on us, already says everything, and says it without a word: the man on the phone had understood that much, with his dog.

You do not translate a gaze. You receive it, or you flee it.

Translation would not give us the animal. It would give us ourselves — and that is the one thing we run from. To leave the eagle an eagle, the crow a crow, the animal what it is: that is not giving up on understanding. It is the only way to look at it long enough, and true enough, for it to stay.
