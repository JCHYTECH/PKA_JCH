# AI Will Never Speak the Language of the Living
## From the Voynich Manuscript to birdsong — why the machine sees patterns, not meaning

*The machine sees patterns. Meaning requires a shared world. Between a six-hundred-year-old book no one can read and a birdsong no one can translate, there is the same frontier — and it is not technological.*

---

Every two or three years, a headline returns: "AI has deciphered the Voynich manuscript." Every time, the scholarly community rejects it. In 2018, two Canadian researchers announced they had recognized Hebrew encoded as anagrams. The claim circled the globe, then collapsed — a methodological artifact, never validated by peers. In 2026, the official status is unchanged: the Voynich remains undeciphered.

But the Voynich is not the story. It is the symptom of a deeper limit — one that runs through ancient manuscripts, the songs of whales, and the question we have always asked: **can we "speak" with the living, and at what cost?**

## 1. The manuscript that resists

The Voynich is a real book. Six hundred years old, two hundred pages, plants that do not exist, a script that belongs to no known language. For years, AI has dissected it: symbol frequencies, word structure, position on the page. The results are strangely precise — the word distribution is not random; it follows rules close to a natural language. And yet: no translation.

It is not alone. Linear A, the Rongorongo of Easter Island, the Indus script, Proto-Elamite, the Phaistos Disc — the whole club of undeciphered scripts has passed through the algorithms. Without result.

The rule that emerges is clean: **AI decodes when there is a known language and a large corpus.** Linear B, cuneiform, Greek — there it excels. The dazzling proof is the Herculaneum papyri: scrolls carbonized by Vesuvius in 79 AD, unreadable for two thousand years, "virtually unrolled" by X-rays and machine learning. In June 2026, an entire scroll was read for the first time. But those were Greek texts — a language we already know how to read. The machine did not "translate"; it made legible what the ink had erased.

When the underlying language is unknown, there is no Rosetta Stone, no translation, no key. And an algorithm cannot invent a key from nothing.

## 2. The same limit, in the animal world

This is exactly the wall facing research on animal language.

In 2024, a study proposed a "phonetic alphabet" for sperm whales. The structure is there — repeated, segmentable, measurable patterns. The meaning stays closed. It is the Voynich in acoustic form: **structure is cheap, meaning is not.**

A larger corpus does not help. Project CETI accumulates masses of acoustic data, just as we hold thousands of Indus inscriptions. What is missing in both cases is not data — it is a **key**, a verifiable link between a signal and its referent.

A study from Tel Aviv University (2026) showed this bluntly. Researchers tested neural networks on the vocalizations of toddlers, whose meaning humans understand at least in part. The result: the AI grouped together sounds of **different meaning**, and separated sounds of **identical meaning**. It recognized patterns, not significance.

## 3. The brain is not enough either

One might think that measuring brain activity solves the problem. It is a whole field, and the model organism is the zebra finch: researchers have reconstructed song from neural activity alone. One lab even decodes these birds' *dreams* — asleep, they sing melodies different from the ones they sing awake.

But here is the crucial nuance: this work decodes **motor production** — how the brain commands the muscles that make the sound. Not the meaning. Knowing how a sound is made says nothing about what it means. You can read the score without hearing the music.

## 4. The umwelt, or the flower we never see

There is something more unsettling. We reason from what we perceive — and we perceive little.

The bee sees ultraviolet guides on the flower that we do not see. Humans hear from 20 Hz to 20 kHz; the bat emits up to 120 kHz, the dolphin 150 kHz, the elephant descends to 5 Hz. For decades we missed entire channels: the ultrasonic calls of rodents, the infrasound of elephants — discovered late, simply because our instruments cut off at the edge of our own hearing.

The biologist Jakob von Uexküll gave this a name: the **umwelt** — the perceptual world proper to each species. And the sound spectrum is only one axis. There is chemistry (pheromones, a continent we barely smell), electricity (electric fish that "speak" in electric fields), magnetism (the navigation of birds), substrate vibration (spiders). Each modality is a wavelength we risk forgetting — like analyzing a flower in RGB and never switching to ultraviolet.

## 5. Can AI deduce an unknown umwelt?

Not directly. AI works on data; without a sensor there is no data, therefore no inference. It cannot guess the existence of a signal no instrument has ever measured. Bird magnetoreception was not discovered by analysis, but by experiment.

But AI can do something subtler: **signal that a channel is missing.** This is the logic that let us infer exoplanets (we did not see them; we saw the anomaly) or dark matter (an unexplained gravitational residue). Faced with two organisms synchronizing with no known channel, an algorithm can detect the unexplained correlation — the hole in the explanation. That is a prediction of an umwelt, not a discovery. Characterizing the medium remains a matter of physics and sensors.

The loop, though, is real: measure wide → detect a residue → hypothesize a channel → build a sensor → begin again. AI is a link in it, not the engine.

## 6. What the ancients knew

This desire to understand animals is not new. The Quran (27:16) says Solomon and David were "taught the language of the birds" — the root of Attar's *Conference of the Birds*, the twelfth-century Sufi masterpiece. In China, Gongye Chang, Confucius's son-in-law, was said to understand the birds. Hinduism knows a *siddhi*, a yogic power: the "knowledge of the sounds of all beings." Shamanisms make it a pillar.

And there is a real, scientifically documented version of this dialogue: the **greater honeyguide**, an African bird, and the Yao honey-hunters of Mozambique. The Yao call the bird with a precise cry — "brrr-hm," passed from father to son — and the bird leads them to a wild bees' nest. The human harvests the honey, the bird eats the wax. Claire Spottiswoode proved experimentally that the bird distinguishes this exact call from any other sound: it **triples** the odds of being guided.

The lesson is precise: this is not translation. It is **co-evolutionary mutualism** — two species sharing a signaling system, neither "decoding" the other. The traditions had turned this dream into myth; nature made it practice.

## 7. The law of dialect

One intuition remains, the most beautiful of all: birds have song **dialects**. Faced with the same event, the response varies from one region to another. But faced with a **predator** — what becomes of the dialect?

It disappears.

This is the discovery of Peter Marler, a founding figure of modern ornithology. Song is **learned** — hence the dialects. Alarm calls are largely **innate** — hence conserved. And under grave events, selection freezes the signal: if a call drifts, the kin does not recognize it, and it dies.

The extreme case is **convergence across species**: the "seet" call warning of an aerial raptor is remarkably identical in unrelated birds. The reason is acoustic — a high, narrow sound is impossible for a predator to localize. All species converge on the same solution because they face the same constraint. This is Marler's localization hypothesis (1955), reconfirmed in October 2025 by the Cornell Lab: birds separated by continents and millions of years of evolution share a nearly identical alarm call.

The law, in one sentence: **dialect is a luxury of signals that are not life-or-death.** The moment a signal becomes a matter of survival, selection freezes it — and at the limit, fuses it across species.

## What all this teaches

Three things, which are one.

First, **AI is a pattern machine, not a meaning machine.** It can map the structure of the Voynich, the alphabet of sperm whales, the dreams of zebra finches. But moving from pattern to meaning requires a shared world — a common reference — that statistics cannot reconstruct.

Second, **our own umwelt is a trap.** We search for animal "language" with our ears, our eyes, our categories. The bee's flower does not exist in our spectrum. Part of the problem is not that animals have nothing to say — it is that we are not equipped to hear it.

Third, **meaning is not decoded; it is shared.** The Yao hunter and the honeyguide do not translate one another: over generations they have learned to inhabit the same signal. Perhaps that is the real lesson — the one Solomon, Gongye Chang, and the shamans already knew, and that the algorithms remind us of despite themselves: understanding the living is not a matter of calculation, but of belonging.

WildLens was built on a similar refusal: to look long enough, and precisely enough, that something stays. The machine can map what is measurable. The rest — the meaning — belongs to the one who stays still, listens, and is willing to be looked back at.
